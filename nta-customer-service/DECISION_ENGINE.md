# NTA Customer Service Decision Engine

This document expands the reasoning standard used by `nta-customer-service`.

## 1. Facts before conclusions

Build a short timeline from the strongest available evidence. Label each point as one of:

- `CONFIRMED` — directly supported by Shopify, tracking, photos/video, listing text, or both parties.
- `CUSTOMER CLAIM` — stated by the customer but not independently verified.
- `NTA CLAIM` — stated by NTA but not independently verified.
- `INFERENCE` — a reasonable interpretation that is not directly proven.
- `UNKNOWN` — material information that is missing.

Do not upgrade a claim or inference into a confirmed fact merely because it fits the preferred explanation.

## 2. Responsibility classification

Use the narrowest classification supported by the evidence.

### NTA
Use when the evidence shows the problem arose from NTA's product, description, packing, handling, fulfilment, advice, or other controllable action.

### Carrier
Use when the evidence shows the problem arose during delivery and the carrier is the dominant cause. NTA may still owe the customer a remedy depending on the legal and contractual position, so carrier responsibility is not automatically the same as no NTA responsibility.

### Customer
Use only when there is persuasive evidence that post-sale handling or another customer-controlled action caused the problem. A possible husbandry issue is not enough by itself.

### Shared
Use when both NTA and customer/carrier actions materially contributed and the evidence supports both contributions.

### Undetermined
Use when more than one plausible cause remains and the available evidence cannot fairly distinguish them.

When uncertain, prefer `UNDETERMINED` over inventing certainty.

## 3. Evidence weighting

Give the most weight to contemporaneous, objective records:

1. Shopify order/refund/fulfilment records.
2. Photos/videos with relevant timing/context.
3. Product listing and policy wording at the time of purchase.
4. Tracking or carrier records.
5. Written customer/NTA messages created at the time.
6. Later recollections.

In-person inspection is relevant evidence about what was visible at handover. It does not prove that a latent defect did not exist and does not remove statutory rights.

## 4. Policy and ACL check

Ask four questions:

1. What did NTA promise or represent?
2. What did the customer actually receive?
3. Did the customer follow any material, clearly communicated conditions or instructions?
4. Could Australian Consumer Law provide a remedy regardless of NTA's internal policy?

Do not use policy wording as a shield when the underlying representation or product appears defective. Do not cite legal conclusions from memory in a contentious or high-value case; verify current official guidance first.

## 5. Remedy ladder

Choose the lowest level that fairly resolves the actual problem.

### Advice / monitoring
Appropriate when no failure is established, the item remains usable/healthy, and additional observation is genuinely useful.

### Correction / clarification
Appropriate for listing wording, instructions, or minor information errors where the customer has not suffered a material loss.

### Store credit
Use only where fair and lawful, and not as a substitute for a refund when the customer is legally entitled to one.

### Partial refund
Appropriate when only part of the supplied value failed or the customer keeps an item with a measurable shortfall.

### Replacement
Appropriate when replacement directly cures the problem and is proportionate.

### Full refund
Appropriate where the evidence and applicable consumer rights support undoing the transaction or where another remedy would not fairly resolve the failure.

### No remedy
Appropriate only where the evidence supports NTA's position. Explain the factual reason; do not rely on dismissive policy language.

## 6. Devil's-advocate test

Before finalising, answer:

- If I were the customer, what is the strongest factual argument against this decision?
- Is NTA relying on an assumption it cannot prove?
- Did NTA's wording, conduct, or advice contribute to the misunderstanding?
- Is the proposed remedy materially smaller than the demonstrated loss?
- Are we using a store policy in a way that could conflict with ACL?
- Would the written response look reasonable if later reviewed by a third party?

If the answer exposes a weakness, revise the classification, remedy, or wording.

## 7. Confidence scale

- `LOW` — critical facts missing or evidence conflicts strongly.
- `MODERATE` — plausible decision, but an important uncertainty remains.
- `MODERATE-HIGH` — evidence points clearly one way with limited uncertainty.
- `HIGH` — objective records strongly establish the material facts and remedy.

Confidence describes the evidence, not how strongly David feels about the case.

## 8. Customer-response structure

A strong reply usually contains four parts:

1. Acknowledge the concern.
2. State the relevant observed facts without overclaiming.
3. Explain the decision or immediate next step.
4. State what will happen if the situation changes or new evidence appears.

Keep the tone human and concise. Do not expose internal labels such as `CUSTOMER`, `UNDETERMINED`, or confidence ratings unless useful.

## 9. Learning loop

After a case is resolved, ask whether it revealed a preventable process gap. Examples:

- unclear listing wording
- poor photos
- ambiguous shipping options
- missing acclimation instructions
- weak order notes
- unclear refund timing
- repeated customer misunderstanding

Fix the upstream process when the cost is reasonable. A customer-service engine that only writes replies but does not improve the system is incomplete.
